Abstract
Omnidirectional or 360 cameras provide embodied AI agents, with a holistic,
wide field-of-view (FoV) sensing of 3D structures of their surroundings. This
advantage has sparked significant interest in leveraging emerging Multi-modal
Large Language Models (MLLMs) for omnidirectional spatial reasoning. However,
as MLLMs are predominantly trained on 2D perspective images, they struggle
with the severe distortions and wrap-around discontinuity inherent to spherical
geometry. Consequently, enabling MLLMs to generalize to non-Euclidean 3D
spaces without retraining remains a significant open challenge. In this paper, we
propose SPHMIND, a novel, training-free, and plug-and-play framework that
explicitly bridges this gap. Our key idea is to decouple semantic perception from
geometric reasoning: rather than forcing MLLMs to internalize complex spherical
laws, we preserve their strong semantic priors and handle the geometry externally.
To achieve this, we introduce a Spherical Harmonics-based Spatial Graph (SHSG)
to map spatial relationships using equivariant transformations. We then couple this
with Inference-Time Geometric Grounding (IGG), a model-agnostic, closed-loop
optimization process that aligns the MLLM’s internal representations with these

Introduction
Omnidirectional images (ODIs)2 have gained attention due to their holistic 180 × 360 field-of-
view (FOV) [29, 46]. ODIs provide global spatial awareness beyond the restricted front-facing
view of conventional inputs [39] important for embodied robotic systems operating in complex 3D
environments [15, 18]. While 360 cameras provide the context required for 3D scene understanding,
interpreting ODIs remains highly challenging. Despite advances in Multi-modal Large Language
Models (MLLMs) on perspective 2D images [31, 8, 6, 17], these models suffer severe degradation on
ODIs. The core issue is a spatial-semantic gap [43, 48]: MLLMs are essentially “flat” world models
trained on rectilinear images in R2. Their architectures rely on 2D Euclidean mechanisms such as
translation-invariant convolutions and grid-based attention [42, 13, 24]. In contrast, ERP projection
introduces non linear distortions and spherical periodicity creates cross-seam boundaries. [42, 13, 24].
Thus, omnidirectional reasoning requires overcoming two coupled challenges: 1) modeling con-
tinuous non-Euclidean spherical geometry,and 2) injecting these constraints into MLLMs without
overriding semantic priors. Recent approaches adapt to ODIs via specialized fine-tuning or spherical-
to-2D patch decomposition [12, 36, 28]. However, fine-tuning is data-intensive and generalizes poorly,
while patch decomposition breaks spherical topology and weakens global spatial relationships.
To address the challenges, our key idea is to decouple semantic perception from geometric reasoning.
Instead of imposing rigid Euclidean assumptions encoded into MLLM backbones [23], we delegate
complex spatial transformations and constraints to geometric operations. This frees the MLLM to
focus on what it does best: semantic understanding. while treating geometric grounding as continuous
constrained optimization rather than a discrete post-processing heuristic. As shown in Fig. 1(a),
current VLMs ambiguously assign any object to the right of an anchor as “right”, whereas grounding
on the sphere via SH yields a distinct peak at the correct answer with lower scores for distractors.
We propose SPHMIND, a training-free framework with two key components.. First, to understand
non-Euclidean geometry, we introduce the Spherical Harmonics-based Spatial Graph (SHSG) to
enable reasoning directly on spherical surfaces. Within SHSG, spatial relations are formulated as
continuous directional tensor fields rather than discrete pairwise links. By encoding object nodes
using spherical harmonics (SH), we preserve directional asymmetry (i.e., A →B̸ = B →A) and
achieve rotational equivariance [22]. Second, to dynamically bridge the semantic-geometric gap,
we develop Inference-Time Geometric Grounding (IGG) (Sec. 3.2). IGG optimizes the MLLM’s
final-token hidden state to minimize a differentiable geometric violation energy, steering the latent
representation toward geometric consistency while preserving pretrained visual priors enabling
geometrically-grounded, zero-shot decoding without weight updates.
We evaluate SPHMIND on a 360 spatial VQA benchmark of ∼12K question-answer pairs derived
analytically from Matterport3D [37] and Stanford2D3D [7]. Across three MLLM backbones,
without any training (Fig. 1b), SPHMIND achieves a mean gain of +21.4% overall, +20.5% on
directional reasoning, and +18% on distance understanding over the MLLM baselines. Under
azimuthal rotations of the panorama origin, SPHMIND achieves ×5.9 full rotational consistency
over the ERP-pixel baseline, confirming that spherical harmonic scoring produces stable predictions
regardless of spherical orientation. We further evaluate zero-shot on ODI-Bench [44], which provides
human-annotated directional queries on real-world 360 data, and OpenView-VQA [10], showing
that our framework is a generalized spherical understanding mechanism and adapts to similar spatial
reasoning tasks without any dataset-specific tuning. Finally, Fig. 1(a) shows a qualitative real-world
example on an in-the-wild 360 image, where SPHMIND correctly resolves a directional query that the

Related works
Omnidirectional Scene Understanding. Modern VLMs [31, 8, 6] excel at narrow FoV images,
their performance degrades significantly on ODIs. The standard ERP introduces latitude-dependent
distortions that violate the Euclidean assumptions inherent in common 2D architectures [42, 13,
24, 3, 4]. Consequently, rotation-invariant spatial concepts (e.g., left/right, near/far) become highly
distorted and difficult to represent reliably on a planar grid. To address these geometric challenges,
SoTA methods typically rely on extensive fine-tuning or specialized architectural designs. Sphere-
aware models, such as SphereUFormer [9] and Distortion-Aware Transformers [47, 2, 52, 51, 50],
modify internal attention operators to respect spherical topology. Other holistic 3D pipelines leverage
frequency-domain representations; for instance, HUSH [30] demonstrates that spherical harmonics
provide a robust, geometry-aligned basis for panoramic scene understanding directly on S2 rather than
on distorted planar parameterizations [19]. However, these existing approaches demand expensive
fine-tuning and specialized backbone modifications to adapt. Figs. 9 and 2 show that models trained
on perspective images apply the same assumptions to ERP inputs, leading to inconsistencies with the
true spherical geometry and misinterpretation of directional relations and distances.
Omnidirectional Spatial Reasoning and Benchmarking. Early omnidirectional reasoning methods,
such as VQA 360◦[12], adapted planar pipelines by projecting ODIs into cubemap faces to handle
ERP distortion and provide a attention fusion mechanism. While practical, this discrete decompo-
sition fragments the continuous spherical manifold, severely degrading global spatial relationships
that depend on uninterrupted 360◦context. With the rise of MLLMs, benchmarks including Om-
niVQA [49] and Pano-AVQA [45] introduced sphere-aware embeddings to enhance local recognition.
Nevertheless, performance drops remain substantial for complex spatial tasks such as relative direc-
tion and distance estimation. Recent diagnostic studies [20, 33] confirm these persistent weaknesses,
revealing a critical spatial-semantic gap: MLLMs excel at semantic encoding yet fail to preserve
geometry under non-Euclidean topology. In contrast, our SPHMIND introduces a fundamentally
different paradigm. Rather than forcing a MLLM to implicitly learn spherical geometry through
exhaustive retraining, we explicitly decouple semantic perception from geometry.

Adapting to Frozen MLLMs. Training-free approaches often rely on post-hoc heuristics such as
confidence filtering, re-ranking, or localization-based de-hallucination [11], but MLLMs can remain
overconfident in physically impossible layouts [26, 27], and late-stage filtering cannot repair an
internally inconsistent spatial state [38, 21]. Our approach instead aligns with controlled generation
and inference-time steering: plug-and-play methods such as PPLM [16] show that frozen models
can be guided by optimizing latent activations. GrAInS [34] aims to enable interpretable, token-
level control over VLMs via Integrated Gradients to shift from undesirable hallucinations toward
truthful responses. Subsequent works show that latent intervention can improve faithfulness and
control [40, 25]. Crucially, SPHMIND extends this paradigm from latent steering to explicit geometric
control to realize two advantages.Unlike prior methods that either fragment the visual space through
geometric decompositions or require costly geometry-specific fine-tuning , SPHMIND bridges this
gap in a training-free manner by reasoning directly on the spherical manifold.
3
Methodology
Overview. Our central idea is to decouple semantic understanding from geometric reasoning. Put
simply, rather than forcing VLMs to internalize spherical geometry, we preserve their semantic
priors and externalize geometry. Intuitively, we propose a novel SphMind approach, as shown in
Fig. 3. Given an ERP image I and a spatial reasoning question Q, we seek an answer y∗that is
both semantically meaningful and geometrically consistent with the 360 scene. Our framework
achieves this via two tightly coupled novel components. Specifically, Spherical Harmonics-based
Spatial Graph (SHSG) (Sec. 3.1) builds a rotation-consistent scene representation directly on S2
by encoding objects and their spatial relations as spherical harmonic features, producing Y , a set
of geometrically plausible VQA answers for y∗. However, a pretrained VLM operating on I and Q
remains unaware of how to understand this geometric signal and correct its internal representation.
Inference Time Geometric Grounding (IGG) (Sec. 3.2) bridges this gap by injecting the SHSG
geometric constraints directly into the VLM’s final hidden state h0. Thereby, IGG steers this h0
towards geometrically grounded distribution while preserving the semantic priors. In short, the VLM
answers what is present; SHSG and IGG enforce the VLM on where and how the objects relate on
the sphere. Now we describe these two technical components in details.
3.1
Spherical Harmonics-based Spatial Graph (SHSG) Module
As depicted in Fig. 3, given an ERP image I and a spatial query Q, we localize objects by projecting
I into cubemap faces and lifting each raw detection outputs ˜Gk obtained from object detectors [35]

coordinates (θk, ϕk), and detector confidence ˆck ∈[0, 1]. Note that cross-face duplicates are removed
via geodesic non-maximum suppression (GNMS; Appendix A.2), yielding a duplicate-free spatial
graph G = {Gk}K

3.2
Inference-Time Geometric Grounding (IGG)
SHSG is a purely geometric module: it operates entirely on the sphere and produces a directional
evidence set Sd with SH-based geometric scores with no semantic knowledge. However, the frozen
VLM’s hidden state h0 encodes rich semantic priors but carries no spherical understanding. Therefore,
our IGG bridges this gap by propagating SHSG’s geometric evidence directly into h0 via energy
minimisation, as shown in Fig.5. That is, IGG aims to ground the VLM’s output distribution in the
spherical geometry of the scene prior to decoding without updating any model weights.
Geometric Violation Cost. Given the directional evidence set Sd with SH directional scores
SSH(a, k, d) for each candidate object node Gk, where a higher score reflects stronger directional
alignment, we define the geometric violation cost as the complement of the SH score:
C(yk) = 1 −SSH(a, k, d),

Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets. We evaluate SPHMIND on three benchmarks: (I) Matterport3D (MP3D) and Stanford
2D-3D-S: ∼12k synthetic QA pairs derived from 3D annotations provided across six MP3D scenes
and four 2D-3D-S areas. (II) ODI-Bench: ∼1k human-annotated QA pairs on real-world Flickr ODIs;
we test zero-shot on Relative Direction and Egocentric View Orientation. (III) OpenView-VQA: ∼1k
human-annotated camera-rotation reasoning QA pairs from restricted FoV crops.
Benchmark Construction. For MP3D and 2D-3D-S, ground-truth answers of VQA are derived from
the provided annotations i.e 3D object centroids, semantic labels, and camera poses, projected to
viewer-centric spherical coordinates (θ, ϕ, r) (refer to Appendix C). ODI-Bench and OpenView-VQA
provide human-annotated QA pairs with raw panoramic images and camera poses.
Baselines. We compare SPHMIND across three open-source VLM backbones spanning different
architectures and scales: Qwen2.5-VL (3B, 7B) and LLaVA-1.6 (7B). Each backbone is evaluated
under two settings: Vanilla, where the frozen VLM receives the raw ERP panorama and question,
and +SphMind, where the same frozen VLM is augmented with SHSG+IGG. Presence and counting
are evaluated without external detections to measure the raw ERP perception gap; direction and
distance use identical YOLO detections and graph G for both settings, isolating SHSG+IGG as the
sole variable (see Appendix C.1 for full protocol details).

EQ-1: Can explicit spherical geometric grounding enable frozen VLMs to reason about
panoramic spatial relationships that they fundamentally cannot understand in ERP images?
EQ-2: Does the geometric prior in SPHMIND generalize zero-shot across structurally distinct
benchmarks, invariant to rotations and coordinate conventions without any task-specific training?
EQ-3: Which components of SPHMIND are necessary for the observed gains in spatial reasoning,
and is the S2 spherical representation necessary for panoramic understanding
360° Panoramic VQA. Table 1 reveals that direction baselines expose the most severe failure:
Qwen2.5-3B reaches only 9.5–17.5% across scenes, confirming misinterpretation of directions in
pixel space. Distance baselines cluster at 35–50%, revealing that VLMs actively misread ERP-
distortions. Presence and counting baselines are substantially higher, showing the failure is specific
to geometry-intensive queries rather than general VLM weakness. Gains are consistent across
architectures and scale, ruling out model-specific adaptation.
[A-1] SPHMIND raises overall accuracy from 35.9% to 56.4% (+20.5%). Direction gains are
largest for weakest model (Qwen2.5-3B: ∼12%→∼39%) and smallest for strongest (LLaVA-
1.5-7B: ∼31%→∼48%) proving increased geometric understanding rather than a semantic bias
being corrected. Distance is consistently around 60% for all models and scenes.
Zero-Shot Transfer to ODI-Bench. Table 2 (a) compares SPHMIND against four prompt-engineering
strategies on real-world Flickr ODIs. All methods remain within ±1.5% of the 46.1% RD base-
line, with crop grounding actually degrading to 44.2%, confirming that linguistic or input-space
interventions cannot substitute for incorrect geometric understanding.
Rotation Angle Grounding on OpenView-VQA. Table 2(b) tests whether exact spherical conversion
of camera rotation angles outperforms language based rotation interpretation. OpenView-VQA
expresses spatial targets via rotation angles from restricted FoV crops; a frozen VLM associates these
with candidates via language statistics rather than geometric conversion. SHSG converts the rotation
angle directly to (θtgt, ϕtgt) = (θcam + ∆ψ, ϕcam + ∆ρ) and substitutes it as the anchor, with all
other operations unchanged. (refer to Appendix G).

Conclusion and Future Work
Figure 7: t-SNE embedding of the SHSG
spherical field at 450 samples on the sphere
colored by direction.
In this work, we introduced SPHMIND, a training-
free and plug-and-play framework for spatial reason-
ing in 360◦images. Our central observation is that
existing VLMs are designed for reasoning on pla-
nar images, and therefore struggle to reason reliably
over spherical scene geometry. SPHMIND addresses
this mismatch by externalizing geometric reason-
ing through a Spherical Harmonics-based Spatial
Graph (SHSG) Module and Inference-Time Geo-
metric Grounding (IGG), enabling frozen VLMs
to produce geometry-consistent predictions without
retraining or architectural modification.
Future Work: Looking forward, we aim to extend
SPHMIND to dynamic and multi-view settings for
embodied navigation, incorporate complementary
modalities such as depth for richer 3D reasoning.
We hope this work motivates further research on
geometry-grounded panoramic reasoning.

Claim.

B.4
Step Size and Convergence
The step size is fixed as η = ε0 = 0.15 with early stopping when |∆E| < δ = 0.005. Unlike the
pointwise energy formulation, the contrastive gradient is self-scaling: its magnitude is proportional
to the log-ratio log(Pk−/Pk+), which is large when correction is most needed and diminishes as
the ordering converges. Empirical behavior. In practice, early stopping fires at T=1 for 74% of
examples, confirming that a single step is empirically sufficient for the majority of cases; this is an
empirical observation and not a consequence of the Lipschitz descent bound above. This eliminates
the need for an adaptive step schedule or multi-step tuning — a single Riemannian step suffices
for the majority of examples, with early stopping providing a principled termination criterion. The
energy satisfies a standard Riemannian descent guarantee for Lipschitz-smooth objectives:

Validity of geometry-derived spatial relations.
A natural concern is whether 3D-centroid-derived
spatial relations correspond to visually verifiable directions in the panoramic image. We address this
in two parts.
Co-presence. Since our pipeline operates on full equirectangular panoramas (360◦× 180◦), every
object within the distance filter is guaranteed to appear somewhere in the image regardless of its
angular position relative to the anchor. Co-presence in a narrow field-of-view crop is not required and
is not assumed by our model.
Occlusion. GT generation does not perform ray-casting and therefore cannot verify that a geometri-
cally valid target is optically visible. This limitation is shared by all existing 3D-annotation-derived
indoor VQA benchmarks. We mitigate its impact through three design choices: (i) the 6 m target
distance filter removes objects likely to be at low resolution or partially hidden behind intervening
structure;Anchor instances beyond ranc = 6 m from the camera and target objects beyond rtgt = 6 m
are excluded. These thresholds approximate the largest dimension of a single indoor room in both
datasets — objects beyond this distance are almost certainly in an adjacent space and therefore not
spatially related to the anchor in the sense intended by the question. (ii) the multi-anchor voting
scheme requires a candidate to score above threshold τ = 0.05 across multiple anchor instances
— a transiently occluded object will generally receive inconsistent votes and fail to accumulate
sufficient support; (iii) restricting the object vocabulary to eleven large furniture categories (≥0.3 m
characteristic size) reduces the incidence of complete occlusion compared to small-object categories.
We further conducted a manual audit of 50 randomly sampled spatial questions, verifying that in
46 of 50 cases both anchor and target are visually discernible in the equirectangular panorama at
the labelled angular relationship. The four failure cases all involved the sofa category partially
occluded behind a chair cluster at close range (< 1.5 m), and were not systematically biased toward
any direction label. This 92% visual validity rate is consistent with figures reported for comparable
geometry-derived benchmarks.

Distance Query Handling
E.1
Problem Formulation
The distance benchmark (Appendix C) poses exclusively pairwise camera-distance queries of the
form “which of object A or object B is closer to the camera?” Ground-truth answers are derived from
camera-frame radial distances rk = ∥vk∥computed directly from 3D mesh annotations (Appendix C).
The task therefore requires estimating the sign of rA −rB from image evidence alone, without access
to ground-truth depths. Unlike directional queries, distance queries involve no anchor-candidate
relationship — both objects are candidates and neither serves as a spatial reference; the reference
point is the camera origin.

OpenView-VQA Evaluation Details
G.1
Benchmark and Scope
OpenView-VQA was designed to evaluate out-of-view (OOV) spatial reasoning from restricted FoV
crops — inferring objects in unobserved directions from partial observations. SphMind does not
perform OOV reasoning: it requires the target object to be detectable in the input and makes no claims
about inferring unobserved scene content. We include this evaluation to test a distinct generalization
axis: whether exact spherical conversion of camera rotation angles provides a geometric advantage
over language-prior-based rotation interpretation in frozen VLMs. For questions where the target is
genuinely out of view and undetectable in the crop, performance is bounded by detection recall and
does not reflect the capability being evaluated here.
G.2

G.3
What the Gain Measures
The +18.7% gain over the vanilla baseline reflects a specific failure mode of frozen VLMs: when a
rotation angle such as “turn left 30◦” is presented as text, the VLM associates it with candidates via
language co-occurrence statistics rather than converting it to an exact spherical direction. SHSG’s
conversion θtgt = θcam + ∆ψ is exact arithmetic — the gain comes entirely from replacing language-
prior rotation interpretation with geometric rotation parsing. The gain is larger for pure yaw queries
(+12.0%), where ∆ψ maps directly to an azimuthal shift, and smaller for pitch queries (+8.0%),
where elevation changes are harder to resolve when detections are sparse in the vertical extent of the
restricted crop.
This result confirms that geometric rotation parsing — not language priors — is the bottleneck for
rotation-based spatial queries in current frozen VLMs, and that exact spherical arithmetic resolves
this bottleneck without any task-specific training.
G.4
Comparison to Trained Specialist
The trained specialist achieves 46.0% by learning OOV reasoning from OpenView’s training distri-
bution — it can infer objects in directions entirely absent from the crop. SphMind achieves 39.7%
by solving a structurally different and narrower subproblem: exact rotation angle grounding on the
detectable subset of questions. These two numbers are not directly comparable as measures of the
same capability. The appropriate comparison is SphMind (39.7%) versus the zero-shot VLM baseline
(21.0%), which share the same scope — neither performs OOV reasoning — and the +18.7% gain
isolates the contribution of geometric rotation parsing alone.

Rotational Consistency Score: Protocol and Analysis
H.1
Dataset Construction
We construct the RCS evaluation set from Stanford 2D-3D-S from each Area scenes, which contains
around 85 spatial question groups in each scene covering four azimuthal directions (front, left, right,
behind) and object categories (chair, desk, sofa, table, board, bookcase, door). Each question group
consists of a spatial question of the form “What is to the [direction] of the [anchor]?” paired with a
GT answer object, an anchor object, and a reference panorama.
For each group, three rotated variants are generated by applying horizontal ERP shifts ψ ∈
{90◦, 180◦, 270◦} to the input panorama and updating the direction label via the cyclic map:
d′ = ROTψ(d),
ROT90◦: {F→R, R→B, B→L, L→F}
(41)
where F=front, R=right, B=behind, L=left. The GT answer object is identical across all four variants
— only the panorama origin and direction label change.
H.2
YOLO Detection and Intersection Filtering
YOLO is run independently on each of the four ERP variants via cubemap projection (six 512×512
perspective faces per panorama). Only questions where both anchor and target labels appear in
the detection set for all four rotation variants are retained; this intersection filter removes ∼35% of
question groups on average, leaving n=55 groups used for RCS evaluation.
Although YOLO detects the same object categories across rotations, projected bbox centroids differ
slightly as objects shift across cubemap face boundaries. Empirically, we observe ∼2–5◦variation in
projected (θ, ϕ) coordinates of consistently detected objects across rotation variants. Since SHSG
scores using Wigner-D inner products with SH resolution ≈20◦at Lmax = 7, the 2–5◦position jitter
is an order of magnitude smaller than the scoring bandwidth and does not affect directional decisions
ERP-Pixel direction zones, by contrast, use absolute pixel offsets with hard boundaries at ±12.5% of
image width (≈256px for a 2048-wide ERP); a 5◦positional shift (≈28px) near a zone boundary can
flip an object’s assigned direction, directly causing RCS failure. This asymmetry — SHSG robust to
position noise, ERP-Pixel fragile to it — is an architectural difference, not a confound.
