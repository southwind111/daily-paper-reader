Abstract. Reconstructing high-fidelity surfaces of dynamic scenes re-
mains a critical challenge. While current methods excel at novel view
synthesis, they often struggle to recover accurate and stable geometry,
particularly under large non-rigid deformations. This results in noisy
meshes that are unsuitable for downstream simulation or editing. In this
work, we introduce a new paradigm for dynamic surface reconstruction
based on a parametric Signed Distance Function (p-SDF). Our key in-
sight is to generalize static SDF fields—where each spatial point stores a
constant value—into time-dependent parametric curves where each curve
models a temporally evolving SDF trajectory. This parametric SDF rep-
resentation provides a principled way to capture complex temporal varia-
tions, naturally enforcing smoothness and continuity in shape dynamics.

Introduction
Reconstructing high-fidelity surfaces from multi-view video is a long-standing
pursuit in computer vision, powering applications in simulation, virtual and aug-
mented reality (VR/AR), autonomous robotics, and film production. Achieving
this remains highly challenging, as dynamic scenes often involve complex non-
rigid motions, large-scale dynamics, and intricate topological changes.
Recent advances in scene representations, such as Neural Radiance Fields [23]
and 3D Gaussian Splatting [13], have dramatically advanced novel view synthesis
(NVS). Their dynamic extensions [1,7,9,26,38] further enable photorealistic NVS
across time. Nevertheless, these methods are designed primarily for appearance
modeling, often producing noisy or temporally unstable underlying geometry.
Two lines of work have explored ways to improve the quality of dynamic sur-
face reconstruction. The first strategy adopts a Lagrangian perspective, tracking
3D trajectories through deformation fields. This involves mapping a canonical
template to the dynamic scene via global deformations [19, 42] or incremental
deformations [2,35,43]. By explicitly disentangling appearance from motion, this
approach maintains temporal consistency across frames. However, it is inherently
limited by the fixed-topology template and the smoothing effect of deformation
MLPs, making it difficult to handle topological changes and highly complex
non-rigid dynamics. To bypass explicit tracking, the second strategy adopts an
Eulerian perspective by factorizing the 4D spatio-temporal volume into plane or
tensor features [9, 28]. Without explicit motion modeling, the representation is
unconstrained by continuous motion functions, enabling it to handle topologi-
cal changes or discontinuous events. However, without temporal regularization,
mesh quality often becomes unstable under complex dynamics.
In this work, we propose parametric Signed Distance Function(p-SDF), a
novel dynamic scene representation that reconstructs high-fidelity surfaces across
time. Our key intuition is that while 3D shapes deform, the evolution of the
Signed Distance Function (SDF) at any fixed spatial coordinate follows a con-
tinuous 1D trajectory (see Fig. 2; refer to Appendix Sec. A for a more detailed

Specifically, we represent dynamic geometry using a grid where each vertex
stores basis function parameters: low-order polynomials for global deformations
and Fourier bases for high-frequency details. At any timestamp, a static SDF field
can be queried to extract an explicit mesh via differentiable iso-surfacing [30].
Separately, an efficient temporal tri-plane appearance field models the scene’s
appearance by predicting PBR material properties. The resulting mesh and ma-
terials are then fed into a differentiable renderer for end-to-end optimization
against 2D images. The explicit disentanglement of geometry, materials, and
lighting constrains the network to reconstruct physically accurate surfaces.
Our p-SDF provides a principled and efficient solution for dynamic 3D recon-
struction. We evaluate our method on synthetic and real-world datasets, demon-
strating improvements over prior approaches in geometric accuracy. Moreover,
the high-quality material decomposition from our framework readily supports
relighting applications, enabling physically plausible relighting effects.

Related Work
2.1
Neural 3D Representations
Recent breakthroughs in Neural Radiance Fields [23] and 3D Gaussian Splat-
ting [13] have revolutionized static novel-view synthesis. However, their primary
focus on appearance modeling often comes at the expense of geometric accuracy.
NeRF-based methods leverage coordinate-based networks to represent volume


4
C. Gao et al.
density and color. To extract usable geometry, several works incorporate signed
distance functions (SDFs) as underlying representations [34,35,40], enabling sur-
face recovery via Marching Cubes [20]. Gaussian-based methods represent scenes
as explicit 3D primitives. To bridge the gap between discrete points and con-
tinuous surfaces, techniques such as surface-alignment regularization [4,5,8,10],
Poisson reconstruction [5,8], and SDF fusion [3,11] have been introduced. How-
ever, existing extraction pipelines, such as Poisson reconstruction or SDF fusion,
typically fail to maintain stable topology or a fixed vertex count across dynamic
frames. Despite these advancements in static reconstruction, extending these
representations to dynamic scenarios remains a formidable challenge.
2.2
Dynamic Surface Reconstruction
The central challenge in dynamic surface reconstruction is modeling temporal
evolution while preserving spatial fidelity. Existing approaches generally follow
three paradigms: Global Deformation [19, 25, 26, 37, 38, 42], Incremental Opti-
mization [2,35,43], and 4D Space-Time Volumes [1,9,22,28].
The global deformation paradigm establishes a mapping between a static
canonical space and dynamic observations at time t. Many methods in this cate-
gory are designed for monocular settings where each timestamp provides a single
image from a randomly sampled viewpoint. For instance, DG-Mesh [19] deforms
canonical 3D Gaussians into target frames and recovers surfaces via per-frame
Poisson reconstruction. Dynamic 2DGS [42] refines this by employing surface-
aligned 2D surfels with TSDF fusion. However, these methods are fundamentally
limited by the fixed topology of their canonical templates. Furthermore, their
deformations rely on continuous MLPs that can oversmooth temporal motion.
We adapt these baselines to the multi-view setting for fair comparison.
Instead of a global mapping, incremental optimization reconstructs scenes
frame-by-frame, focusing on deformations between adjacent timestamps. Ap-
proaches like NeuS2 [35] and AT-GS [2] typically propagate weights from the
previous frame to the current one. While capable of handling large motions and
topological changes, these methods are constrained by their reliance on auxiliary
supervision, such as optical flow [43], to curb error accumulation.
The 4D space-time volume paradigm decomposes the 4D domain into effi-
cient spatio-temporal representations. Methods like Tensor4D [28] factorize 4D
volumes into planes to represent density and color at any (x, y, z, t). As a highly
flexible paradigm, it bypasses explicit motion modeling and fixed templates,
enabling the recovery of complex dynamics and topological changes. However,
lacking strong structural priors, 4D volumes suffer from temporal flickering.
Similar to our work, SDFFlow [22] models SDF deformations but relies on
an integral formulation that is computationally expensive and prone to error
accumulation. This makes it unsuitable for long sequences and high-fidelity re-
construction. In contrast, our method directly parameterizes the SDF trajec-
tories with basis functions, inherently enforcing continuity regularization. The
static SDF at a given timestamp is efficiently obtained by querying the function,
avoiding costly explicit integration that accumulates errors.

Methodology
We now describe our method. The key idea is to generalize static Signed Dis-
tance Function (SDF) fields into parametric SDF trajectories. Below, we first
review background concepts on SDF and temporal signal modeling in Sec. 3.1.
We then provide an overview of the framework in Sec. 3.2, followed by detailed
descriptions of our parametric SDF formulation (Sec. 3.3) and appearance mod-
eling (Sec. 3.4). Lastly, we discuss the implementation details in Sec. 3.5.

3.2
Framework Overview
Motivation. Representing dynamic scenes requires modeling how surfaces evolve
over time. As illustrated in Fig. 2, our key observation is that surface motion in-
duces smooth temporal evolution of the SDF values at any fixed spatial location,
i.e., s(x, t) traces a continuous 1D signal. These continuous 1D signals can be
efficiently parameterized via a collection of basis functions. This parameteriza-
tion naturally enforces temporal coherence in the SDF evolution while retaining
flexibility for large deformations and topological changes.
Pipeline. Fig. 3 illustrates the full system. Given a timestamp t, we first obtain
per-node SDF values by evaluating basis functions and then extract an explicit
mesh using differentiable iso-surfacing [30]. Concurrently, to model appearance,
we apply a temporal tri-plane appearance field and query PBR attributes at
each mesh vertex. A physically based differentiable renderer then synthesizes
the target views; gradients backpropagate through geometry, appearance, and
rendering, enabling end-to-end optimization (Sec. 3.5).

Discussion. This parametric surface-mesh design provides several important
benefits: (i) the basis formulation inherently enforces temporal coherence, while
the combination of polynomial and Fourier bases captures both large-scale defor-
mations and fine-grained high-frequency motions, offering robustness in complex
dynamic scenes; (ii) we model the geometry by storing per-node curve parame-
ters on a grid, which enables efficient and scalable mesh extraction by directly
querying the stored function, thus bypassing the computational overhead of per-
point MLPs in prior works. (iii) by using the explicit mesh for rendering within
the differentiable rendering-optimization loop, we apply direct supervision to
the geometry. This ensures a consistent and detailed surface, avoiding the noisy
artifacts common in purely implicit methods like AT-GS [2].

computational complexity compared with directly modeling 4D appearance; (ii)
with precise geometry, this simple representation can disentangle shape and ap-
pearance and produce high-quality material and lighting estimates.
3.5
Implementation Details
Optimization Pipeline. The input to p-SDF consists of multi-view RGB videos,
foreground masks, and calibrated camera intrinsics/extrinsics. At each iteration,
we query p-SDF at time t, extract a mesh with differentiable FlexiCubes [30],
rasterize it with nvdiffrast [15], query PBR attributes at visible (x, t) surface
points, and render the target view with a differentiable PBR shader following
Nvdiffrec [24]. Gradients from the RGB and mask losses, together with regular-
izers, are backpropagated end-to-end to the geometry, appearance, and lighting
parameters.
Loss Function. We employ a compact objective that balances photometric re-
construction with geometric and appearance priors. The overall loss is:
  \mat h cal {L } _{\tex t {total}} = \mathcal {L}_{\text {photo}} + \mathcal {L}_{\text {curve}} + \mathcal {L}_{\text {app}}, 
(8)

where Lphoto enforces consistency between rendered and ground-truth images,
Lcurve regularizes the spatial smoothness of SDF trajectories to ensure coher-
ent motion for neighboring vertices, and Lapp regularizes material and lighting.
Detailed formulations of these losses are provided in Appendix Sec. B.
Model Details. We represent p-SDF with a 963 grid, 6 polynomial basis func-
tions, and a set of Fourier components whose number is chosen per scene from
the range [18, 100] based on motion complexity. For the Temporal Tri-plane Ap-
pearance Field, we adapt the multi-resolution hash grids following Grid4D [37],
with spatial resolution spanning from 16 to 2048 and temporal resolution from
8 to the full sequence length.
Training and Optimization. We build our framework upon the open-source Radi-
anceFieldStudio codebase [39] and conduct all experiments on a single NVIDIA
RTX 4090 GPU. We train each scene for 5,000 iterations, which takes approxi-
mately 1 to 3 hours. We optimize the model using an Adam optimizer [14] with
exponential decay.

Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets. We evaluate our method on three diverse datasets to demonstrate its
robustness across synthetic and real-world scenarios with complex dynamics:
SynMotion-360. We first curate a new synthetic benchmark designed to eval-
uate dynamic surface reconstruction under large, non-rigid deformations that
existing datasets rarely capture. Specifically, we select 7 animated assets from
Objaverse [6,17], each comprising 70–300 frames rendered via Blender from 38
viewpoints. For each sequence, 32 views are used for training and 6 for testing.
Additional dataset visualizations are provided in Appendix Sec. J.
DiVa-360. This is a real-world multi-view dataset featuring dynamic tabletop
scenes captured by synchronized RGB cameras [21]. While it provides synchro-
nized videos and foreground masks, ground-truth meshes are unavailable. We
evaluate our method on 4 representative sequences, using 31 cameras for train-
ing and 6 held-out views for evaluation.
CMU Panoptic Studio. This widely used real-world dataset captures human
dynamics [12]. Following the protocol in SDFFlow [22], we focus on challenging
multi-person interactions. Each sequence consists of 24 frames recorded from 10
calibrated cameras, and all views are used for training.
Metrics. We evaluate performance from two aspects: geometric reconstruction
accuracy and novel-view synthesis quality. Following [30], geometric quality is
quantified using Chamfer Distance (CD) and F-Score (F1) for spatial alignment,
while Edge Chamfer Distance (ECD) and Edge F-Score (EF1) assess boundary
fidelity and topological connectivity. To assess mesh structural integrity, we re-
port the percentage of sliver triangles using Aspect Ratio (AR) and Minimum
Angle (MA) criteria: the AR metric counts triangles with aspect ratio above 4.0,

and the MA metric counts triangles with minimum angle below 10◦. Regarding
synthesis quality, we report PSNR, SSIM [36], and LPIPS [41] to capture
pixel-level accuracy, structural similarity, and perceptual realism, respectively.
Held-out test images are used only for novel-view synthesis evaluation; geometry
metrics are computed from ground-truth meshes or point clouds when available,
and from mesh-quality statistics otherwise.
Baselines. We evaluate our method against state-of-the-art (SOTA) baselines
from two predominant paradigms: Global Deformation and Incremental Opti-
mization. For the former, we compare with Dynamic 2DGS [42], which uses
surface-aligned 2D surfels with TSDF fusion, and DG-Mesh [19], which performs
per-frame Poisson reconstruction from deformed Gaussians. For the latter, we
include NeuS2 [35], a representative SDF-based method using per-frame hash
grids, and AT-GS [2], which employs neural transformation caches for frame-by-
frame propagation. While SDFFlow [22] is conceptually related to our trajectory
modeling, it is excluded due to its prohibitive optimization costs and error ac-
cumulation as discussed in Sec. 2. GauSTAR [43] requires additional depth su-
pervision, and DynaSurfGS is not included because its code was unavailable at
the time of evaluation. All reported baselines are trained in the same multi-view
video setting. For monocular baselines [19,42], we extend their data loaders to ag-
gregate supervision from all training cameras at each timestamp; the main tables
report these multi-view-adapted results. A controlled monocular-vs.-multi-view
comparison is provided in Appendix Sec. F. This diverse selection of baselines
supports a rigorous assessment of our model’s capacity to handle complex dy-
namics and high-fidelity dynamic mesh reconstruction.

Further Analysis
Ablations. We analyze basis composition, grid resolution, and Fourier bandwidth
in Appendix Tables A4–A6. Fourier bases are critical for dynamic geometry: the
polynomial-only variant reaches only 25.91 dB PSNR, Fourier-only improves to
35.90 dB, and the full mixed basis reaches 36.34 dB. For spatial discretization,
963 provides our default quality-cost trade-off between the coarse 643 grid and

the costlier 1283 grid. For temporal bandwidth, smooth-motion Toy saturates at
lower Nf, whereas fast-motion Lego benefits from larger bandwidth.
Computational Cost. We report training cost, peak VRAM, and inference speed
in Appendix Tables A9 and A10. p-SDF uses more VRAM (19.7 GB) because
its dense parametric grid stores coefficients for the full temporal SDF field, but
its training time remains practical at 121 min on an RTX 4090. At inference,
p-SDF runs at 11.4 FPS, slower than Gaussian baselines due to mesh extraction
and PBR material evaluation, but faster than NeuS2 (8.6 FPS).
Topology Changes. We provide topology-change evidence in Appendix Figs. A1
and A2. In a torus-to-sphere sequence, p-SDF fits the sharp SDF sign transition
caused by hole closure and reconstructs the topology change from multi-view
observations. This behavior follows from modeling per-location SDF trajectories
directly, without relying on a fixed-connectivity canonical template.
Large Motion and Temporal Smoothness. Appendix Sec. E evaluates large-motion
robustness and temporal interpolation. On the fast-motion Lego sequence, the
motion-velocity-versus-CD analysis shows that p-SDF maintains lower geomet-
ric error in high-speed intervals and reconstructs more complete surfaces. Under
an even-frame training and odd-frame testing protocol, p-SDF also achieves the
lowest interpolation CD on both smooth Toy and fast-motion Lego, supporting
the temporal consistency of the learned SDF trajectories.
Monocular Setting. Appendix Sec. H further evaluates p-SDF under the monoc-
ular protocol. A qualitative comparison with representative monocular baselines
shows that p-SDF remains competitive for smooth dynamics, while additional
analyses reveal noisy surfaces and degradation under large motion. These results
clarify that the temporal SDF prior helps under weak supervision, but calibrated
multi-view consistency remains essential for high-fidelity geometry.
Additional Results. Appendix Sec. I provides additional qualitative Tensor4D
comparisons and NHR dataset results, complementing our evaluation with a 4D
volume baseline and human-centric multi-view captures.

Conclusions and Limitations
Conclusions. In this paper, we introduced parametric Signed Distance Function
(p-SDF), a novel dynamic scene representation that leverages parametric basis
functions to represent SDF trajectories. p-SDF naturally enforces temporal co-
herence while remaining flexible to large deformations and topological changes.

Combined with physically based appearance modeling, our framework yields
accurate geometry and robust material decomposition in highly dynamic condi-
tions, and supports downstream applications such as relighting. Experiments on
synthetic and real-world calibrated multi-view videos show that p-SDF estab-
lishes state-of-the-art geometric accuracy while maintaining competitive novel-
view synthesis quality. We believe p-SDF offers a promising direction for dynamic
surface reconstruction and its applications.
Limitations. Our method relies on multi-view consistency. In extremely sparse
scenarios (e.g., monocular data), reconstruction accuracy inevitably degrades,
although the analytical basis functions of p-SDF provide a smooth prior that
prevents global structural collapse. Detailed analysis of this monocular setting
is provided in Appendix Sec. H. As an Eulerian representation, p-SDF models
the temporal evolution of SDF values at stationary grid nodes; thus, it does
not inherently provide point-to-point correspondences, such as vertex tracking,
across frames. Nevertheless, the temporal continuity of our basis functions natu-
rally supports smooth temporal interpolation and slow-motion synthesis. These
properties and their implications are analyzed in Appendix Sec. E.
