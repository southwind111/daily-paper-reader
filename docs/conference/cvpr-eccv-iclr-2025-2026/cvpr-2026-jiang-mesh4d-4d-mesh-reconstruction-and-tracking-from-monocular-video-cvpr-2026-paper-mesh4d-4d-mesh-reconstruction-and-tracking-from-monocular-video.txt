Abstract
We propose Mesh4D, a feed-forward model for monocular
4D mesh reconstruction. Given a monocular video of a dy-
namic object, our model reconstructs the object’s complete
3D shape and motion, represented as a deformation ﬁeld.
Our key contribution is a compact latent space that encodes
the entire animation sequence. This latent space is learned
by an autoencoder that, during training, is guided by the
skeletal structure of the training objects, providing strong
priors on plausible deformations.
Crucially, skeletal in-
formation is not required at inference time. The encoder
employs spatio-temporal attention, yielding a more stable
representation of the object’s overall deformation. Build-
ing on this representation, we train a latent diffusion model
that, conditioned on the input video and the mesh recon-
structed from the ﬁrst frame, predicts the full animation in
one shot. We evaluate Mesh4D on reconstruction and novel
view synthesis benchmarks, outperforming prior methods in
recovering accurate 3D shape and deformation.
1. Introduction
In monocular 4D shape reconstruction, we are interested in
reconstructing the 3D shape and motion of a dynamic ob-
ject captured in a monocular RGB video. 4D reconstruction
has many applications in vision, graphics, and robotics. For
example, it can be used to automate the creation of 3D ani-
mations, which is costly and time-consuming to do by hand,
and requires domain expertise. However, 4D reconstruction
is very challenging as the monocular video contains limited
information about the 3D shape and motion of the object,
which instead must be recovered in full, beyond what is vis-
ible in the video.
Often [16, 35, 77] this problem has been tackled using
analysis-by-synthesis, where an animated 3D shape is opti-
mized iteratively to ﬁt the input RGB(D) frames. However,
these methods can only reconstruct the visible portion of
the object and often produce noisy results due to occlusions
and ambiguity. Recent feed-forward methods [11, 19, 44]
have emerged as promising alternatives. These networks
learn to reconstruct in 3D a frame of a monocular video, as

well as the scene motion between that frame and another.
However, each application of the network only recovers the
3D points that are visible, and their motion only between
the two frames rather than across the whole sequence. As
a result, they are insufﬁcient to recover the complete 4D
structure of an animated object.
Reconstructing and tracking the parts of the geometry
that are not visible in the video requires strong 3D and phys-
ical priors that can only be learned from data. This calls
for latent 3D generative models, which have been shown to
capture rich priors on 3D shapes [20, 46, 62], at least in the
static case. In this paper, we thus ask whether such gen-
erative models can be extended to solve 4D reconstruction,
implicitly capturing cues such as symmetry and smoothness
that can help to recover the 3D shape and its motion beyond
what is directly visible in the video. Recent contributions
such as GVFD [73] have already explored this idea and in-
troduced versions of 3D generators [46, 62] that can handle
dynamics. However, their ultimate aim is to generate good-
looking images of the object and they are less concerned
with accurately recovering the 3D shape and motion.
In this work, we introduce Mesh4D, a feed-forward
model that learns to accurately reconstruct 3D objects and
their motion from a monocular RGB video (see illustra-
tion Fig. 1). We represent the object’s shape as a 3D mesh
extracted from the ﬁrst frame of the input video, and then
generate a deformation ﬁeld that displaces the surface of
the mesh to represent the deformation of the object over
time. This representation is intuitive as it factorizes the 3D
shape and motion. By comparison, others [18, 41, 54, 69]
have approached 4D reconstruction by outputting indepen-
dent 3D reconstructions of each frame and thus do not ex-
plicitly model the object’s motion. Explicitly modeling mo-
tion is often crucial, for example, to texture the 3D object
consistently over time.
We argue that, in order to accurately recover the motion
of objects, animations should be modeled holistically from
the beginning to the end of the video. To make this feasi-
ble, we introduce a new Variational Auto-Encoder (VAE)
to encode the shape deformation in a compact latent space.
Prior work like Motion2VecSet [5] has proposed analogous
encodings, but only for two frames at once. In contrast,
we show that a latent space representing the motion of the
object throughout the whole sequence performs better. To
this end, we build a transformer [50] encoder that uses
spatial, temporal, and global attention modules [14, 51] in
each block, capturing long-term spation-temporal correla-
tions among points.
We also argue for using motion-related information to
guide VAE training. In particular, we propose using the ob-
ject’s skeleton, as a cue on the space of possible deforma-
tions of the object. As shown in Fig. 2, we leverage both
skinning weights and bone information as additional inputs
to the encoder, which signiﬁcantly improves the quality of
the learned latent space. Importantly, the skeleton is privi-
leged information which is not needed at inference time.
Another challenge in monocular 4D mesh reconstruction
is the lack of suitable 4D datasets for training and bench-
marking. Following recent works [18, 20, 32, 65], we do not
train our deformation model from scratch, but start from a
static 3D generator [46]. Because the latter is pretrained on
large datasets of 3D objects, it helps to achieve robustness
and generalization to a wide variety of object types. We
then use a dataset of animated 3D objects (with annotated
3D tracks) for training and evaluation. As there are few such
datasets, we build one by ﬁltering 3D animated assets from
Objaverse [9], selecting high-quality dynamic 3D meshes
with skeletons. For evaluation, we propose a new bench-
mark focusing on the quality of 3D shape and motion recon-
struction rather than just the visual quality of the rendered
video, which has been the focus of some prior work [5]. To
do so, we collect 50 animated 3D assets with signiﬁcant ob-
ject motion, high-quality textures, and no overlap with our
training data.
To summarize, our contributions are as follows. First,
we propose a new model, Mesh4D, for monocular 4D mesh
reconstruction. This model reconstructs the 3D shape and
deformation of an object from a monocular video. Second,
we build our model on top of a new VAE that encodes the
object’s deformation from the beginning to the end of the
sequence in a compact latent space, suitable for latent diffu-
sion. We then train a diffusion model to generate the motion
of the object observer in the input video. Third, we propose
a benchmark to assess monocular 4D shape reconstruction
models with a focus on 3D reconstruction quality instead of
2D rendering quality.
2. Related work
2.1. Optimization-based 4D reconstruction
Iterative or optimization-based methods reconstruct from
monocular or multi-view videos by iteratively ﬁtting a 4D
representation to them [4, 10, 12, 25, 26, 35, 37, 39, 48, 53,
56, 58, 67, 68]. With the advent of neural radiance ﬁelds
(NeRFs) [33] and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3D-GS) [21],
many time-dependent NeRFs [4, 10, 12, 25, 26, 37, 39] and
dynamic 3D Gaussian Splatting methods [53, 56, 58, 67, 68]
have been proposed for 4D reconstruction. Although sig-
niﬁcant progress has been made, most approaches are eval-
uated on simple scenarios with quasi-static scenes [37, 58]
and relatively small datasets [13, 22, 70].
To demonstrate effectiveness on diverse scenarios, recent
works explore priors from large-scale pretrained models.
One popular direction is to generate multi-view videos with
pretrained video diffusion models [1–3, 15] and then per-
form 4D reconstruction via per-scene optimization [59, 63,
14525


71]. Another line of work [8, 17, 27, 40, 74] directly dis-
tills 4D knowledge from large-scale diffusion models us-
ing slow score-distillation sampling iterations [38]. How-
ever, these methods focus on “plausible” novel view syn-
thesis (NVS), rather than accurate geometry and tracking.
More recently, feed-forward 3D generators, such as Hun-
yuan3D [45–47], TripoSG [24], Trellis [62], and Step1X-
3D [23], have shown impressive results on diverse and
complex scenarios, but they focus only on static 3D re-
construction. Building upon these feed-forward 3D gen-
erators, V2M4 [6] reconstructs 4D meshes from monoc-
ular videos by generating per-frame 3D meshes indepen-
dently, followed by an optimization step to ensure tempo-
ral coherence. However, they still require per-scene opti-
mization to achieve consistent 4D reconstruction, which is
time-consuming and less ﬂexible. In contrast, our approach
directly reconstructs temporally coherent 4D meshes along
with tracking from monocular videos in a feed-forward
manner.
It generalizes to diverse scenarios without per-
scene optimization and is therefore much more efﬁcient.
2.2. Feed-forward 4D reconstruction
Like our method, feed-forward 4D reconstruction directly
infers a 4D representation from monocular videos in a sin-
gle pass. MonST3R [75] effectively reconstructs dynamic
scenes by adapting the static 3D reconstruction produced by
DUSt3R [55] with dynamic scene supervision. Numerous
follow-ups take a similar path [11, 19, 44], adding func-
tionalities like tracking.
However, they mainly work on
pairwise frames or stereo videos. To handle long monoc-
ular videos, Cut3R [54] introduces a memory bank to
store spatial and temporal information, enabling continu-
ous static and dynamic scene reconstruction from a stream
of monocular images. ω3 [57] further improves scalability
by building a permutation-equivariant architecture on top of
VGGT [51]. Beyond DUSt3R-based methods, Geo4D [18]
leverages video generators [64] to infer 4D point maps
from monocular videos. 4DGT [66] proposes a transformer
model to predict dynamic 3D Gaussian representations [21]
from monocular videos. However, these approaches mainly
focus on visible geometry reconstruction, whereas we ad-
dress the more challenging task of complete 4D mesh re-
construction with tracking. This is harder due to the need
to infer the invisible parts of surfaces and establish dense
correspondences over time.
Some recent works also explore complete 4D recon-
struction from monocular videos in a feed-forward manner.
L4GM [41] leverages a pretrained ImageDream [52] to gen-
erate missing-view images, which are then used to train a
feed-forward 4D Gaussian reconstructor. GVFD [73] em-
beds the 4D mesh data into a Gaussian Variation Field us-
ing a compact latent space, and then trains a video-to-4D
Gaussian model by inferring latent codes from monocular
videos. However, they still focus on producing plausible
novel view synthesis, rather than aiming for accurate ge-
ometry. More closely related to our task, concurrent work
ShapeGen4D [69] encodes a 4D mesh sequence into a se-
quence of latents and then learns a diffusion model to pre-
dict the latents directly, conditioned on monocular videos.
However, they align the latents only with a shared set of
query points from the ﬁrst frame and do not directly predict
dense correspondences. Instead, our Mesh4D is based on
a novel spatio-temporal Transformer architecture operating
directly on 4D meshes, which can effectively capture tem-
poral coherence and establish dense correspondences over
time. Moreover, compared to Motion2VecSets [5] which
processes two meshes at a time, Mesh4D encodes sequences
of 4D meshes jointly, and directly predicts complete 4D
meshes from monocular videos.

3.1. Preliminaries: Latent 3D reconstruction model
Following recent works on 3D diffusion [18, 20, 30, 32], we
build Mesh4D on top of a pre-trained latent 3D reconstruc-
tion model for static objects. We use Hunyuan3D 2.1 [46],
an excellent model learned from millions of 3D data sam-
ples, but any other similar model could be used instead.
The model uses a VAE to map the 3D mesh M to a com-
pact latent code zs based on the VecSet representation [72].
The encoder zs = Es(P; n) computes the latents by ﬁrst
uniformly sampling a point cloud P →RM→3 from the
mesh M, augmented with their normals n →RM→3, and
then applying a neural network to it. Given the code zs, a
decoder Ds(Q; zs) recovers the 3D shape of the object by
computing its signed distance function (SDF) in correspon-
dence of a set of 3D grid query points Q →R(H→W →D)→3.
These are ﬁnally converted to a triangle mesh via the march-
ing cubes algorithm [31].
Given the VAE, 3D reconstruction amounts to learning
a conditional denoising diffusion generator that can sample
the latent code zs ↘p(zs|I) given the image I. This model
is trained by minimizing the ﬂow matching objective [29]:

3.3. Deformation diffusion model
As shown in Fig. 3, given the reconstructed canonical mesh
M1 and the input video I, we use a diffusion model to gen-
erate the deformation latent zd ↘p(zd|M1, I) conditioned
on both signals. To build the deformation diffusion model,
we extend the shape diffusion model in Sec. 3.1 so that it
learns a velocity ﬁeld vd
ω with additional temporal embed-
dings and spatial embeddings p1 →RN→3 sampled from
M1, as well as extra attention layers in each DiT block
to fuse temporal video cues and canonical shape informa-
tion. Video features are extracted from DINO-Giant [36]
frame by frame and cross-attended by the corresponding la-
tent tokens. Similar to GVFD [60], the spatial embedding
p1 endows the initial noise with spatial awareness, which
improves spatial consistency. During training, we use the
positions of the FPS-sampled sparse features h↗↗as spa-
tial embeddings to align each latent target with its embed-
ding. During inference, we perform FPS sampling on the
reconstructed canonical mesh to obtain the required spatial
embedding. Different from GVFD [60], we also incorpo-
rate temporal embeddings for better temporal consistency
and leverage the extracted high-dimensional shape feature
zs from the canonical mesh for detailed conditioning. The
training objective matches Eq. (2).

4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental settings
Dataset. We start from the curated version of Objaverse-
1.0 [9] released by Diffusion4D [28], where objects ani-
mated with limited motion or large distortions are ﬁltered
out. We extract the skeleton, skinning weights, and the se-
quence of meshes with corresponding vertices. We then ﬁl-
ter out objects with an excessively large number of vertices
or bones, retaining approximately 9k instances. Each in-
stance is rendered as a frontal video with up to 100 frames.
For testing, we select a disjoint set of 50 sequences with sig-
niﬁcant object motion and high-quality textures. We render
four ﬁxed-view videos for each sequence at azimuth angles
0↘, 90↘, 180↘, 270↘. One view is used as the input and the
other three for novel view synthesis (NVS) evaluation.
Baselines. We compare our method with three state-of-
the-art latent 3D reconstruction models: Hunyuan3D 2.1
(HY3D) [46], L4GM [41], and GVFD [73]. Since HY3D is
an image-to-3D reconstruction method, we run it frame by
frame with shared noise to improve temporal consistency.
For L4GM and GVFD, we take the centers of Gaussian
primitives with opacity greater than 0.01 as a point cloud
for geometry evaluation. To align the reconstructed mesh
with the ground-truth mesh, we perform Coherent Point
Drift (CPD) [34] to optimize the scale and rigid transfor-
mation of the ﬁrst-frame mesh. As shown in Tabs. 1 and 2,
entries with the Aligned annotation indicate that the aligned
canonical shape is used as input to our deformation diffu-
sion model, whereas entries without this annotation indicate
that alignment is performed immediately before evaluation.
Metrics. For frame-wise geometry evaluation we report
volumetric IoU, point-to-surface distance (P2S), and Cham-
fer distance. For tracking, we measure the Euclidean dis-
tance between corresponding points (nearest neighbors on
the ﬁrst frame) on the predicted and ground-truth meshes
(ϖ2-Corr). For novel view synthesis (NVS) [7, 76], we report

4.4. Ablation study
We ablate key design choices of the deformation VAE. We
train two variants: one without skeleton information and
one without temporal and global attention. At test time we
use the ground-truth ﬁrst-frame mesh as the canonical mesh
and evaluate the reconstructed deformation ﬁeld. As shown
in the orange box of Fig. 6, removing skeleton information
impairs rigid transformations, resulting in a twisted stick.
As shown in the purple box, removing temporal and global
attention causes jittery motion and larger errors near the
feet. These observations align with the quantitative results
in Tab. 3, conﬁrming the effectiveness of our VAE design.
5. Conclusion
We introduced Mesh4D, a feed-forward model for 4D mesh
reconstruction from monocular videos. Starting from a la-
tent 3D reconstruction model pre-trained on a large collec-
tion of static objects, we add a new VAE that encodes the
object deformation in a compact latent space. We further
introduce a method that uses skeleton information to super-
vise this VAE and a diffusion model built on top of these
components. Thanks to this design, Mesh4D predicts the
full 3D shape of the object as well as its deformation, track-
ing vertices throughout the entire video sequence.
On the Objaverse benchmark, Mesh4D achieves state-
of-the-art reconstruction quality for geometry, motion, and
appearance, while reducing temporal artifacts relative to
competing approaches. Our ablations highlight the value
of our contributions, including using skeletal cues and
spatio-temporal attention in the VAE architecture. Limita-
tions include reliance on a high-quality canonical mesh and
skeletons for training, the inability to represent topological
changes in the mesh, and difﬁculty in reconstructing very
non-rigid objects. Code, models, benchmark splits, and the
full evaluation framework are available.
