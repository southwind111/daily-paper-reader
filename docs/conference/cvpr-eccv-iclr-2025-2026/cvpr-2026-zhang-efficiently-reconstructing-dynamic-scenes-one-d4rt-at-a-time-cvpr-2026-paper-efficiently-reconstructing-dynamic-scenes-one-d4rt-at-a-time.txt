## Abstract 

Understanding and reconstructing the complex geome-try and motion of dynamic scenes from video remains a formidable challenge in computer vision. This paper in-troduces D4RT , a simple yet powerful feedforward model designed to efficiently solve this task. D4RT utilizes a uni-fied transformer architecture to jointly infer depth, spatio-temporal correspondence, and full camera parameters from a single video. Its core innovation is a novel querying mech-anism that sidesteps the heavy computation of dense, per-frame decoding and the complexity of managing multiple, task-specific decoders. Our decoding interface allows the model to flexibly and independently probe the 3D position of any point in space and time. The result is a lightweight and highly scalable method that enables remarkably effi-cient training and inference. We demonstrate that our ap-proach sets a new state of the art, outperforming previous methods across a wide spectrum of 4D reconstruction tasks. We refer to the project webpage for animated results. 3

## 1. Introduction 

Traditional 3D reconstruction asks: ‘What is the geometry of everything, everywhere, all at once?’ We argue this ex-haustive, rigid approach is fundamentally ill-equipped for a dynamic world. Despite the clear need for unified 4D un-derstanding, leading approaches often tackle the problem by dividing it into discrete, task-specific components. For instance, MegaSaM [32] relies on a complex mo-saic of off-the-shelf models to separately estimate mono-depth, metric depth, and motion segmentation. Fusing these disparate signals requires computationally expensive test-time optimization to enforce geometric consistency. ## 2. Method 

D4RT is based on a simple encoder-decoder architecture inspired by the Scene Representation Transformer [42, 43]. As shown in Fig. 2, the video is first processed by a power-ful encoder producing the Global Scene Representation F .The role of the encoder is to capture information about the full environment, identifying dense correspondence across all video frames, as well as understanding the flow of time and its effect on the scene. In a second stage, a lightweight decoder queries F through a unified interface. Specifically, given a 2D point in a source frame , the decoder predicts the 

3D position of the point at a given target timestep (defining the temporal state) and expressed relative to a given camera 

reference (defined by the frame timestep where the camera viewpoint corresponds to this reference). We highlight three key properties of this design: first, in-dices need not coincide, fully disentangling space and time; second, independent query decoding enables efficient train-ing, inference, and flexible (sparse or dense) decoding; and third, it unlocks diverse downstream applications in a uni-fied manner (Tab. 1). 

2.1. D4RT Framework 

Given a video V → RT ↓H↓W ↓3, the encoder E extracts the latent Global Scene Representation 

F = E(V ) → RN ↓C.

Once F is calculated, it remains fixed throughout the second stage, where the decoder D cross-attends from any number of queries into F . We define a query 

q = ( u, v, t src , t tgt , t cam ),

where (u, v, t src ) correspond to source parameters and 

(ttgt , t cam ) correspond to target parameters. Here, (u, v ) →

[0 , 1] 2 represent the normalized 2D coordinates of a point of interest in the source frame tsrc , while (ttgt , t cam ) →

[1 , . . . , T ] denote the temporal indices of the target timestep and the reference camera coordinate system (illustrated in Fig. 2). Each query q is processed fully independently with the video features F to produce its corresponding 3D point position P:

P = D(q, F ) → R3.

From query to 4D reconstruction. Through a simple vari-ation of queries, our framework allows us to address a broad range of 4D tasks as shown in Tab. 1. For full point cloud reconstruction, the model directly pre-dicts the 3D position of all pixels in the video in a shared reference frame tcam. This alleviates the need for coordinate transformations to map pixels from different video frames into a unified coordinate system using explicit, potentially noisy camera estimates. To derive the relative camera pose between any pair of video frames i, j → [1 . . . qi = ( u, v, i, i, i ), qj = ( u, v, i, i, j ).

The resulting sets {D (qi, F )} and {D (qj, F )} describe the same 3D points in different reference frames. We therefore only need to find the rigid transformation between them, which can be efficiently derived through Umeyama’s algo-rithm [49] that solves a 3↑3 SVD decomposition. To recover intrinsics for video frame i → [1 . . . T ], we construct a set of queries for different k source points 

{(u, v )}, again sampled on a (h, w ) grid. We decode all corresponding 3D positions P = ( px, p y, p z). Assuming a pinhole camera model with a principal point at (0 .5, 0.5) ,we get focal length parameters as follows: 

fx=pz(u ↓ 0.5) /p x, fy=pz(v ↓ 0.5) /p y.

We take the median over the k estimates for robustness. Camera models with distortion ( e.g ., fisheye) can also be seamlessly incorporated by adding a non-linear refinement step on top of the initial estimation [64]. 

2.2. Model Architecture 

Our encoder E is based on the Vision Transformer [11] with interleaved local frame-wise , and global self-attention lay-ers [51]. For simplicity, and to support arbitrary aspect ra-tios, we resize the input video to a fixed square resolution before tokenizing it. To incorporate the original aspect ra-tio, we embed it into a separate token and pass it to the transformer along with the main video tokens.

Each query is separately decoded via cross-attention with the Global Scene Representation F , yielding an output feature that is then mapped to a 3D point position P through a simple learned projection. Processing queries indepen-dently through a lightweight decoder is a deliberate design decision with major advantages for training, inference, and general flexibility. During training, only a small, curated se-lection of all possible points needs to be decoded per video for supervision, minimizing both memory requirements and computational overhead. This independence also enables highly efficient inference thanks to trivial parallelization. Furthermore, queries can be chosen freely across all video frames, enabling new inference algorithms (Sec. 2.3) and making D4RT significantly faster than existing methods across several tasks (Fig. 3 and Tab. 3). Interactions between queries would not only pose a computational burden, but also introduce train-test distri-bution shifts, as the model would overfit to the query distribution during training. Indeed, we empirically ob-served major inference performance drops when enabling self-attention between queries in early experiments. Our model achieves spatially and temporally consistent results not through heavy, coupled decoding mechanisms ( e.g ., DPT [40], CoTracker [25]), but through the simple design choice of utilizing a lightweight decoder. This encourages the encoder to reconstruct the scene and implicitly encode the information in the global features F , which serves as an implicit means to enforce global consistency.

## 3. Related Work 

Classical approaches to 3D reconstruction are funda-mentally anchored in multi-view geometry, specifically Structure-from-Motion (SfM) [21, 36] and Multi-View Stereo (MVS) [14, 46]. The standard pipeline exemplified by COLMAP [45] incrementally estimates and optimizes sparse geometry and camera poses. The advantage of these methods is their explicit enforcement of geometric con-sistency and mathematically interpretable reconstructions. However, they are computationally intensive and brittle. 

3.1. Feedforward 3D Reconstruction 

The field of 3D reconstruction has recently shifted to-wards end-to-end, feedforward models that directly infer geometry from images. The seminal work DUSt3R [54] demonstrated that Transformer-based networks [50] can perform 3D reconstruction from unposed and uncalibrated image pairs in an end-to-end approach. VGGT [51] later scaled this approach beyond pairs by using a Vision Trans-former [11] with global attention. Building on this feedfor-ward paradigm, several works have extended the method-ology to dynamic videos [5, 53, 55, 63] and more efficient inference [57, 60]. However, these models share significant limitations. For instance, many do not support changing camera intrinsics within a video [32, 51, 57], and several are restricted to using only the first frame as the camera reference [32, 53, 54]. More importantly, architectures in-spired by VGGT [51] either use separate decoder heads for each task [53, 57, 63] ( e.g ., depth, pose, point cloud), or they incorporate separate models for sub-tasks of 4D recon-struction [34, 47, 55], making the full pipeline cumbersome and computationally expensive to run. Finally, these meth-ods are not directly capable of providing correspondences for dynamic regions of the scene. 7385 Input video MegaSaM [32] ε3 [57] SpatialTrackerV2 [59] D4RT (Ours) …Input video MegaSaM SpatialTrackerV2 D4RT (Ours)

3.2. From 2D to 3D Point Tracking 

The task of point tracking aims to establish long-term 2D correspondences through challenges like occlusions and non-rigid motion, evolving from early methods such as Par-ticle Video [44] to newer deep-learning approaches [1, 2, 9, 10, 19, 66]. This field has further progressed from tracking a sparse set of points to the dense tracking of every pixel in a video [20, 31, 35]. A parallel line of work has explored lifting these 2D tracks into 3D, which is achieved by pro-jecting tracks from image space to camera coordinates using ground truth or predicted depth and camera intrinsics [30]. Most recently, models like SpatialTracker [58, 59], L4P [3], DPM [47] and St4RTracker [13] have moved to directly predicting 3D tracks without priors. Yet, limi-tations remain: St4RTracker [13] and DPM [47] follow the pairwise paradigm of DUSt3R [54], preventing holis-tic video processing. SpatialTrackerV2 [59] is a multi-stage approach and exhibits slow inference speeds as it relies on iterative refinements. Meanwhile, L4P [3] requires differ-ent heads for sparse and dense outputs of the same nature (e.g ., flow vs. 2D tracking or depth vs. 3D tracking). In contrast, our approach unifies these tasks within a single ar-chitecture. Tab. 2 summarizes capabilities across a recent set of state-of-the-art models.

4.1. Qualitative Analysis 

We begin our evaluations by inspecting video reconstruc-tion quality in Fig. 4. Dynamic objects ( e.g ., swan and train) reveal qualitative differences, highlighting perfor-mance gaps between different methods. 4.3. 3D Reconstruction 

We now evaluate the 3D reconstruction capabilities of our model. The most holistic task here is 3D point cloud recon-struction, which requires predicting all observed pixels in the same world coordinate system. We evaluate on two standard benchmarks: MPI Sin-tel [4] and ScanNet [7], which consist of dynamic and static scenes, respectively. For evaluation, we first align the pre-dicted and ground-truth point clouds via mean-shifting fol-lowing Li et al. [32] before reporting the mean L1 distance. As shown in Tab. 5 (left), our model outperforms all recent state-of-the-art models across datasets. 

Depth estimation. We evaluate depth estimation per-formance across four datasets: Sintel [4], ScanNet [7], KITTI [16], and Bonn [37]. Following prior works [32, 53], we align the predicted video depth with the ground-truth depth before computing metrics. Specifically, we adopt two alignment settings: scale-only (S) and scale-and-shift (SS) alignment. Both settings determine a single global scale factor between the predicted and ground-truth depths across the sequence, while the latter introduces an additional 3D translation term, following an affine-invariant setup. After alignment, we compute the Absolute Relative Error (Ab-sRel) [12] between the predicted and ground-truth depths. We show results in Tab. 5 (right). 4.4. Ablation Studies 

We present a series of ablation studies to validate our de-sign choices and hyperparameters. Unless otherwise noted, we adopt a ViT-L backbone with 6 decoder layers as the de-fault setting. The model is trained for 300 k iterations with a batch size 16. All results are reported on the challenging Sintel dataset for both video depth and camera pose. 

Local RGB patch. As described in Sec. 2.2, we introduce an additional embedding that encodes the local appearance around the query’s source location. This additional embed-ding yields dramatic performance improvements as shown in Tab. 7 and Fig. 6. The improvements can be attributed to two factors: (i) the local appearance information helps the query establish more reliable correspondences with the encoded spatiotemporal features; and (ii) these patches pro-vide low-level cues that help to segment objects from their surroundings, leading to fine-grained predictions, as exem-plified by the sharper depth estimates. We demonstrate how the patches unlock subpixel precision in Appendix Sec. C.

## 5. Conclusion 

We introduce D4RT, a simple yet highly scalable feedfor-ward model that reconstructs dynamic 4D scenes with tem-poral correspondence. Our core innovation is an efficient, query-based decoder that allows for the independent pre-diction of any point’s 3D position across time. This flex-ible parametrization avoids the computational bottlenecks of dense per-frame decoders, enabling inference that scales linearly with the number of points to be reconstructed. We demonstrate that D4RT achieves state-of-the-art results across a wide range of 4D tasks including depth, point cloud estimation, and 3D point tracking.
